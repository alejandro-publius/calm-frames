Every number flashmap uses lives here with the exact quoted text it came from and a link to the page it was read on. Nothing in the code may introduce a threshold that is not on this page. If you need a new number, quote it here first.

Fetched on 2026-09-17. Re-read the source pages before quoting them anywhere else.

Two kinds of entry appear below:

  • Verified means the wording is quoted from the primary document and the number is in that wording.
  • Not verified means we could not read the number in a primary document. Those numbers are not used as thresholds anywhere in the code. Where an under-specified part of a spec forced a choice, the choice is written out under "Implementation decisions" and is named in the sidecar so a reader can see what was assumed.

1. WCAG 2.2, Success Criterion 2.3.1 Three Flashes or Below Threshold

Source: Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2, W3C Recommendation. URL: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#three-flashes-or-below-threshold

Verified quote:

Success Criterion 2.3.1 Three Flashes or Below Threshold (Level A) Web pages do not contain anything that flashes more than three times in any one second period, or the flash is below the general flash and red flash thresholds.

Note Since any content that does not meet this success criterion can interfere with a user's ability to use the whole page, all content on the web page (whether it is used to meet other success criteria or not) must meet this success criterion. See Conformance Requirement 5: Non-Interference.

Used for: the pass or fail verdict of flashmap check and of the GitHub Action.

2. WCAG 2.2, definition of "general flash and red flash thresholds"

Source: WCAG 2.2, Appendix A Glossary. URL: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#dfn-general-flash-and-red-flash-thresholds

Verified quote:

general flash and red flash thresholds

a flash or rapidly changing image sequence is below the threshold (i.e., content passes) if any of the following are true:

  1. there are no more than three general flashes and / or no more than three red flashes within any one-second period; or
  2. the combined area of flashes occurring concurrently occupies no more than a total of .006 steradians within any 10 degree visual field on the screen (25% of any 10 degree visual field on the screen) at typical viewing distance

where:

  • A general flash is defined as a pair of opposing changes in relative luminance of 10% or more of the maximum relative luminance (1.0) where the relative luminance of the darker image is below 0.80; and where "a pair of opposing changes" is an increase followed by a decrease, or a decrease followed by an increase, and
  • A red flash is defined as any pair of opposing transitions involving a saturated red

Exception: Flashing that is a fine, balanced, pattern such as white noise or an alternating checkerboard pattern with "squares" smaller than 0.1 degree (of visual field at typical viewing distance) on a side does not violate the thresholds.

Note 1 For general software or web content, using a 341 x 256 pixel rectangle anywhere on the displayed screen area when the content is viewed at 1024 x 768 pixels will provide a good estimate of a 10 degree visual field for standard screen sizes and viewing distances (e.g., 15-17 inch screen at 22-26 inches). This resolution of 75 - 85 ppi is known to be lower, and thus more conservative than the nominal CSS pixel resolution of 96 ppi in CSS specifications. Higher resolutions displays showing the same rendering of the content yield smaller and safer images so it is lower resolutions that are used to define the thresholds.

Note 2 A transition is the change in relative luminance (or relative luminance/color for red flashing) between adjacent peaks and valleys in a plot of relative luminance (or relative luminance/color for red flashing) measurement against time. A flash consists of two opposing transitions.

Note 3 The new working definition in the field for "pair of opposing transitions involving a saturated red" (from WCAG 2.2) is a pair of opposing transitions where, one transition is either to or from a state with a value R/(R + G + B) that is greater than or equal to 0.8, and the difference between states is more than 0.2 (unitless) in the CIE 1976 UCS chromaticity diagram. [ISO_9241-391]

Note 4 Tools are available that will carry out analysis from video screen capture. However, no tool is necessary to evaluate for this condition if flashing is less than or equal to 3 flashes in any one second. Content automatically passes (see #1 and #2 above).

Numbers taken from this quote and used in the code:

1 second the counting period profiles.WCAG.window_ms = 1000
0.10 a general flash transition is 10% or more of the maximum relative luminance (1.0) profiles.WCAG.general.min_delta
0.80 the relative luminance of the darker image must be below this for a general flash profiles.WCAG.general.dark_image_below
0.25 25% of any 10 degree visual field, the area at which condition 2 stops rescuing the content profiles.WCAG.area_fraction_limit

Note on .006 steradians: the spec gives it as another way of saying the same thing ("25% of any 10 degree visual field"). flashmap measures the fraction, not the solid angle, so 0.25 is the number in the code and .006 sr is not used.

The "fine, balanced pattern" exception (white noise, checkerboards below 0.1 degree) is not implemented. flashmap therefore reports some content as failing that the exception would excuse. That direction is the safe one. It is listed in the sidecar as profile.exceptions_not_implemented.

3. WCAG 2.2, definition of "relative luminance"

Source: WCAG 2.2, Appendix A Glossary. URL: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#dfn-relative-luminance

Verified quote:

relative luminance

the relative brightness of any point in a colorspace, normalized to 0 for darkest black and 1 for lightest white

Note 1 For the sRGB colorspace, the relative luminance of a color is defined as L = 0.2126 * R + 0.7152 * G + 0.0722 * B where R, G and B are defined as:

  • if RsRGB <= 0.04045 then R = RsRGB/12.92 else R = ((RsRGB+0.055)/1.055) ^ 2.4
  • if GsRGB <= 0.04045 then G = GsRGB/12.92 else G = ((GsRGB+0.055)/1.055) ^ 2.4
  • if BsRGB <= 0.04045 then B = BsRGB/12.92 else B = ((BsRGB+0.055)/1.055) ^ 2.4

and RsRGB, GsRGB, and BsRGB are defined as:

  • RsRGB = R8bit/255
  • GsRGB = G8bit/255
  • BsRGB = B8bit/255

The "^" character is the exponentiation operator. (Formula taken from [SRGB].)

Note 2 Before May 2021 the value of 0.04045 in the definition was different (0.03928). It was taken from an older version of the specification and has been updated. It has no practical effect on the calculations in the context of these guidelines.

Note 3 Almost all systems used today to view web content assume sRGB encoding. Unless it is known that another color space will be used to process and display the content, authors should evaluate using sRGB colorspace.

Used for: color.srgb_to_linear and color.relative_luminance, exactly as written, including the 0.04045 break point, the 12.92 divisor, the 0.055 offset, the 1.055 divisor and the 2.4 exponent.

4. WCAG 2.2 Understanding 2.3.1: the saturated red arithmetic and the sRGB to XYZ matrix

Source: Understanding Success Criterion 2.3.1: Three Flashes or Below Threshold, W3C Group Note published by the Accessibility Guidelines Working Group as the companion to WCAG 2.2. URL: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html

Verified quote:

Note (new in WCAG 2.2) The new working definition in the field for "pair of opposing transitions involving a saturated red" is a pair of opposing transitions where, one transition is either to or from a state with a value R/(R + G + B) that is greater than or equal to 0.8, and the difference between states is more than 0.2 (unitless) in the CIE 1976 UCS chromaticity diagram. [ISO 9241-391]

The chromaticity difference is calculated as:

SQRT( (u'1 - u'2)^2 + (v'1 - v'2)^2 )

where u'1 and v'1 are chromaticity coordinates of State 1 and u'2 and v'2 are chromaticity coordinates of State 2. The 1976 UCS chromaticity coordinates of u' and v' are calculated as:

u' = 4 * X / (X + 15 * Y + 3 * Z)
v' = 9 * Y / (X + 15 * Y + 3 * Z)

where X, Y, and Z are the tristimulus values of a color in the CIE XYZ colorspace, which can be calculated as:

X = 0.4124564 * R + 0.3575761 * G + 0.1804375 * B
Y = 0.2126729 * R + 0.7151522 * G + 0.0721750 * B
Z = 0.0193339 * R + 0.1191920 * G + 0.9503041 * B

where R, G, & B are values that range from 0-1 as specified in "relative luminance" definition.

This settles a question the normative glossary leaves open. The R, G and B in both R/(R + G + B) and the XYZ matrix are the linear-light values produced by the relative luminance definition in section 3, not the gamma encoded values. flashmap follows that.

ISO 9241-391 itself is behind a paywall and we have not read it. Everything flashmap uses from it is the wording quoted above, which the W3C reproduces.

Verified quote on the size of the 10 degree field and what to analyse:

In WCAG 2.0, a 1024 x 768 screen was used as the reference screen resolution for the evaluation. The 341 x 256 pixel block represents a 10 degree viewport at a typical viewing distance. (The 10 degree field is taken from the original specifications and represents the central vision portion of the eye, where people are most susceptible to photo stimuli.)

With the proliferation of devices of varying screen sizes (from small handhelds to large living room displays), as well as the adoption of CSS pixels as a density-independent unit of measurement, the prior assessment criteria may seem outdated. However, an image of a consistent size uses up relatively the same percentage of a user's visual field on any device. On a large screen, the image takes up less size, but the large screen takes up a larger part of the visual field. On a mobile screen, the image may take up most or all of the screen; however, the mobile screen itself takes up a smaller portion of the user's visual field. So the same dimension of the flashing content, represented in CSS pixels can still provide a consistent means of assessment.

Content should be analyzed at the largest scale at which a user may view the content, and at the standard zoom level of the user agent. For example, with a video that may play in an area of a web page and also at full screen, the video should be analyzed for risks at full screen.

This is why the WCAG area window in flashmap is a fraction of the picture (341/1024 of the width by 256/768 of the height, both very close to one third) rather than a fixed pixel count. It is also why flashmap measures on the picture and not on a scaled-down box inside a page.

Verified quote on other colour spaces:

Where video content is provided in color spaces other than sRGB, the version provided with the highest dynamic range should be tested. In such cases the industry standard definition of a general flash is a change in luminance of 20 cd/m2 or more where the darker image is below 160 cd/m2. (ITU-R BT.1702.) This is applicable for standard dynamic range (SDR) and high dynamic range (HDR) content. For HDR content when the darker state is 160 cd/m2 or more, a general flash is one where the Michelson contrast is 1/17 or greater - where the Michelson contrast is calculated as (LHigh - LLow) / (LHigh + LLow), and where LHigh and LLow are the luminance of the high and low luminance states, respectively.

5. ITU-R BT.1702-3, the broadcast profile (SDR only)

Source: Recommendation ITU-R BT.1702-3 (11/2023), "Guidance for the reduction of photosensitive epileptic seizures caused by television". The full PDF is a free download from the ITU. URL: https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BT.1702/en PDF: https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1702-3-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf

Verified quote, Annex 1, Guideline 1:

A potentially harmful flash occurs when there is a pair of opposing changes in luminance (i.e. an increase in luminance followed by a decrease, or a decrease followed by an increase) as detailed below:

  • When screen luminance of the darker image is below 160 cd/m2, a potentially harmful sequence of flashes occurs where there is a difference of 20 cd/m2 or more between the screen luminance of the darker and the brighter images (see Notes 2 and 3). This is applicable for both standard dynamic range (SDR) and high dynamic range (HDR) programmes.
  • When screen luminance of the darker image is not less than 160 cd/m2, a potentially harmful sequence of flashes occurs where there is a difference of more than 1/17 Michelson contrast (see Note 6). For the purpose of this measurement, the Michelson contrast is defined as (LHIGH - LLOW)/(LHIGH + LLOW) [11], where LLOW is the luminance of darker image and LHIGH is the luminance of brighter image of a flash. This is applicable for HDR programmes only.

Irrespective of luminance, a transition to or from a saturated red is also potentially harmful.

Isolated single, double, or triple flashes are acceptable, but a sequence of flashes is not permitted when both the following occur:

  • the combined area of flashes occurring concurrently occupies more than 25% of the displayed (see Note 4) screen area; and
  • there are more than three flashes (i.e. six changes in luminance as described above) within any one-second period. For clarification, successive flashes for which the leading edges are separated by 360 ms or more are acceptable in a 50 Hz environment or separated by 334 ms or more are acceptable in a 60 Hz environment, irrespective of their brightness or screen area.

Verified quote, Annex 1, Note 3:

NOTE 3 - For the purpose of measurements to check compliance with these guidelines by assuming a relationship between video signal level and screen luminance, SDR pictures are assumed to be displayed with peak white corresponding to a screen luminance of 200 cd/m2 and HDR pictures with Hybrid Log-Gamma (HLG) format are assumed to be displayed with peak white corresponding to a screen luminance of 1 000 cd/m2.

Verified quote, Annex 2, note to Table 1:

Note to Table 1: Video signal level normalized, black at V = 0, to white at V = 1 (Rec. ITU-R BT.1886). For content mastered per Rec. ITU-R BT.709 10-bit digital code values (D) map to V as per the following equation: V = (D-64)/876.

Numbers taken from this quote and used in the broadcast profile:

Number Meaning Where it is used
20 cd/m2 the luminance difference that makes a flash, when the darker image is below 160 cd/m2 profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.general.min_nits_delta
160 cd/m2 the darker image ceiling for the absolute difference rule profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.general.dark_image_below_nits
200 cd/m2 assumed SDR peak white profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.peak_nits default, overridable with --peak-nits
0.25 25% of the displayed screen area profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.area_fraction_limit
3 flashes more than three flashes in any one-second period profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.flash_limit_per_second
2.4 the exponent of the signal-to-luminance curve, see below color.bt1886_nits

The 2.4 exponent is not printed in BT.1702-3. It is the BT.1886 reference decoding function with zero black level, and the recommendation points at BT.1886 for the mapping. We treat the published Table 1 as the primary evidence for it: tests/test_bt1702_table.py asserts that 200 * V ** 2.4, with V taken from the printed 10-bit code value as V = (D - 64) / 876, reproduces all 45 SDR rows of Table 1 to within 0.005 cd/m2, which is the rounding of the two decimal places the table prints. The measured worst case is 0.005 cd/m2, at code value 880. If that test ever fails, the exponent is wrong and the profile must not ship. The full table is transcribed into the test file so the check runs offline.

Not implemented from BT.1702-3, on purpose:

  • The HDR branch (darker image at or above 160 cd/m2, Michelson contrast 1/17). flashmap is SDR only. HLG and PQ inputs are refused rather than analysed with the SDR curve.
  • The 360 ms / 334 ms leading-edge allowance. It can only ever excuse flashes that the three-per-second rule already flagged, so leaving it out keeps the analyser stricter.
  • The overscan allowance in Note 4 (3.5% to 1% of picture width or height).
  • Any persistence time, stripe width or spatial frequency band for Guideline 2. BT.1702-3 gives none, so none is used as a threshold. See section 8 and implementation decision 9.

Guideline 2, potentially harmful regular patterns, IS implemented, in the broadcast profile only, from the numbers in Attachment 1 to Annex 1. That attachment is informative, so section 8 below carries its quote, its status and the consequences of that status, and the sidecar carries the attribution beside every pattern segment. Until 2026-09-17 this list said Guideline 2 was not implemented. It is, so it is not on the list any more.

  • Recommendation ITU-R BT.601-7 (03/2011), "Studio encoding parameters of digital television for standard 4:3 and wide-screen 16:9 aspect ratios". https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BT.601/en https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.601-7-201103-I!!PDF-E.pdf

Verified quote, BT.709-6 Scope and picture characteristics:

Verified quote, BT.601-7 Scope:

This Recommendation also covers the pixel characteristics that represent a 525- or 625-line interlace digital television image.

Verified quote, BT.601-7 Table 1 (the luma coefficients):

Red 1.0 0 0 0.299 Green 0 1.0 0 0.587 Blue 0 0 1.0 0.114

Not verified: the line count at which an untagged stream switches from BT.601 to BT.709. Neither recommendation states a cutoff, because neither is written to cover untagged files. BT.709 is written for 1080 and 720 line HDTV and BT.601 for 525 and 625 line SDTV, and that scope difference is the whole of the argument. flashmap uses a picture height of 720 lines or more as the dividing line. That is our convention, not a threshold from a spec. It is configurable through decode.ColorPolicy.hd_min_height and the choice is recorded in every sidecar as color.matrix_source (inferred-hd-bt709 or inferred-sd-bt601), so a reader can always see when it was guessed rather than read from the file.

flashmap never multiplies these matrices itself. It hands the inferred matrix and range to libswscale through PyAV and lets FFmpeg do the conversion, so the coefficients in use are FFmpeg's, not ours.

8. ITU-R BT.1702-3, Guideline 2 and Attachment 1 to Annex 1: regular patterns

Source, URL and PDF as in section 5, the same document. Read on 2026-09-17 from the PDF, SHA-256 9d1167de5553a82c633020a44b5f729118e90ff6536a3b519f1813e235437bf2. Revision checked the same day: BT.1702-3 (11/2023) is in force; BT.1702-2, -1 and -0 are superseded.

Two conventions in the quotes below, so the repo style rule and the quote can both be kept: the ITU prints its bullet markers as dashes and CLAUDE.md forbids en dashes anywhere here, so the bullets appear as -; and whitespace is normalised from the PDF text layer, which inserts stray spaces inside words. No word, no punctuation and no number is changed.

Verified quote, the scope sentence that governs both guidelines:

To reduce risk, the following guidelines on visual content should be applied when flashing or regular patterns are clearly discernible in normal domestic viewing conditions.

Verified quote, Annex 1, Guideline 2, in full:

Guideline 2 Potentially harmful patterns

Potentially harmful regular patterns occur when an image contains light and dark pairs of clearly discernible stripes in any orientation. The stripes may be parallel or radial, curved or straight, and may be formed by rows of repetitive elements such as polka dots. If the stripes change direction, oscillate, flash or reverse in contrast they are more likely to be harmful than if they are stationary.

Attachment 1 to this Annex provides informative guidance for measurement to identify potentially harmful patterns.

Guideline 2 itself carries no stripe count, no area and no contrast. It defines the hazard and puts every number in Attachment 1.

8.1 Attachment 1 to Annex 1, and what "informative" costs us

Verified as text. Informative in status. The quote is exact and from the primary document, so it meets the bar on this page for a number flashmap may use. What it is not is a requirement: the ITU marks the attachment (informative) and its first sentence says the criteria are used by some administrations, not by all. So everything flashmap reports from it says where it came from, in profile.pattern_rule_source and on every pattern segment, and nothing reported from it is presented as a requirement.

Verified quote, Attachment 1 to Annex 1, in full:

Attachment 1 to Annex 1 (informative)

Guidance on measurement criteria to identify potentially harmful patterns

The following additional guidance on the identification of potentially harmful regular patterns is used by some administrations.

Potentially harmful regular patterning may occur if an image contains more than five light and dark pairs of clearly discernible stripes in any orientation when either of the following two conditions apply:

  • the stripes are stationary and the pattern occupies more than 40% of the displayed screen area; or
  • the stripes change direction, oscillate, flash, or reverse in contrast and the pattern occupies more than 25% of the displayed screen area.

For a pattern to be considered potentially harmful, the difference between the luminance of the darker and lighter bars of the pattern will be the same as the difference between the darker and brighter images of a potentially harmful flash described in Guideline 1 above.

If the patterns obviously flow smoothly across, into, or out of the screen in one direction they are exempt from restriction.

Numbers taken from this quote and used in the broadcast profile:

Number Meaning Where it is used
more than 5 light and dark pairs of clearly discernible stripes, printed as "more than five", so six pairs, which is twelve bars, is the first count that can fail profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.pattern.more_than_pairs
0.40 more than 40% of the displayed screen area, when the stripes are stationary profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.pattern.stationary_area_limit
0.25 more than 25% of the displayed screen area, when the stripes change direction, oscillate, flash or reverse in contrast profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.pattern.changing_area_limit

The bar to bar difference is not a new number. The attachment defines it by reference to Guideline 1, so the pattern rule reads the profile's own general flash rule: for SDR that is a darker bar below 160 cd/m2 and a difference of 20 cd/m2 or more, through the same peak white of 200 cd/m2 and the same BT.1886 curve as section 5. pattern.py is handed profile.general and introduces no luminance constant of its own.

Three things the quote settles:

  1. the stripe count and the area are one test: the count is a gate and then either area condition can trip it;
  2. the contrast condition is separate and additional;
  3. the static and moving cases differ only in the area limit, 40% against 25%. Nothing in the quote gives a duration, a persistence time, a stripe width or a spatial frequency.

On area: BT.1702-3 Note 4 explains "displayed" for Guideline 1 as allowing for overscan of 3.5% to 1% of picture width or height. Attachment 1 says "displayed screen area" without repeating the note. flashmap measures the pattern on the picture after letterbox and pillarbox removal (implementation decision 5) and does not implement the overscan allowance, which makes it stricter. The pattern rule measures the same way, for the same reason.

8.2 WCAG 2.2 does not cover regular patterns at all

Verified, and it is a negative result worth writing down.

Source: Understanding Success Criterion 2.3.1: Three Flashes or Below Threshold, W3C, read 2026-09-17. URL: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html

Every occurrence of the word "pattern" in SC 2.3.1 and its Understanding document is the same exemption, quoted in full in section 2 of this file: the fine, balanced pattern such as white noise or a checkerboard under 0.1 degree. The word "stripe" does not occur. There is no WCAG requirement about stripe counts, static patterns, pattern area or pattern contrast, and the one pattern sentence WCAG has lets content off rather than catching it.

Consequences, and they are firm:

  • the pattern rule is not a WCAG rule. It is absent from the wcag profile, it never changes a WCAG pass or fail, and it must never be described as WCAG conformance in the README, the Devpost text or the video. The map carries summary.flash_pass for the WCAG reading and summary.pattern_pass beside it, and every pattern segment carries "is_a_wcag_finding": false;
  • the house line that room settings can only make things stricter than WCAG is unaffected. A pattern hide is strictly extra.

8.3 Sources that could not be read, and vendor claims that are not sources

  • Ofcom's guidance note on flashing images and regular patterns: read on 2026-09-20, and it has its own section now. See section 8.4. ofcom.org.uk itself is still shut to every method tried, including a real browser engine; the document was read from an Internet Archive capture instead, and section 8.4 says exactly what that costs. No number is taken from Ofcom and nothing in the code cites Ofcom. Worth retrying from a machine with a browser: Ofcom's wording states the contrast condition as its own clause that must apply in addition to the area condition, where the ITU attachment states it as a sentence, and the two read the same way.
  • HardingFPA is a commercial analyser. Its pages are marketing and product support for a closed implementation, not a published threshold, so under CLAUDE.md they cannot supply a number. In particular the claim that a pattern must stay stationary for about half a second before it fails appears only on vendor and third party pages. No primary document read here contains a persistence time for patterns, so flashmap has none.
  • The Japanese NAB and ARIB guidance, which BT.1702-3 alludes to as regional variation. No primary English copy read. Not used.
  • ISO 9241-391, still behind a paywall, as recorded in section 4.

So this is a mirror, not the publisher. The Internet Archive's copy is the provenance, and nobody here has held the file Ofcom serves. That is a weaker chain than every other source on this page and it is the reason this entry says so twice.

Verified quote, paragraph 5:

A potentially harmful regular pattern contains clearly discernible stripes when there are more than five light-dark pairs of stripes in any orientation. The stripes may be parallel or radial, curved or straight, and may be formed by rows of repetitive elements such as polka dots. If the stripes change direction, oscillate, flash or reverse in contrast they are more likely to be harmful than if they are stationary. If the patterns obviously flow smoothly across, into, or out of the screen in one direction they are exempt from restriction.

Verified quote, paragraph 5.1:

Potentially harmful patterns are not permitted when either of the following two conditions apply:

i. the stripes are stationary and the pattern occupies more than 40% of the displayed screen area; or

ii. the stripes change direction, oscillate, flash, or reverse in contrast and the pattern occupies more than twenty five per cent of screen area; and in addition to either of the above two conditions applying, when

iii. the screen luminance of the darker bars in the pattern is below 160 cd.m-2 and differs from the lighter bars by 20 cd.m-2 or more (see notes 1 and 2).

Verified quote, footnote 2, which is where the peak white comes from:

For the purpose of measurements made to check compliance with these guidelines, pictures are assumed to be displayed in accordance with the 'home viewing environment' described in Recommendation ITU-R BT.500 in which peak white corresponds to a screen illumination of 200 cd.m-2.

Four things this settles, and no number moves.

  1. Every number matches BT.1702-3 Attachment 1 exactly: more than five light-dark pairs, 40% stationary, 25% changing, darker bar below 160 cd/m2, difference 20 cd/m2 or more, smooth flow exempt, peak white 200 cd/m2. Nothing in profiles.BROADCAST_SDR.pattern changes and nothing needed to. Two independent documents now carry the same numbers.
  2. The hedge in section 8.3 is resolved. That section guessed that Ofcom states the contrast condition as its own clause applying in addition to the area condition, where the ITU attachment states it as a sentence, and that the two read the same way. They do: 5.1 makes it condition iii, applying "in addition to either of the above two conditions". The guess was right and it is no longer a guess.
  3. It gives no periodicity measure, no spatial frequency band, no stripe width, no duration for patterns, no field of view and no contrast ratio. Checked by reading all six pages. So every number in PatternSettings remains detector tuning and none of them acquires a source. That is a negative result and it is the main thing this document was wanted for.
  4. It does carry one caution, and it is not a threshold. Paragraph 2 says that if the risk of seizures increases with duration, "it is possible that a sequence of flashing images lasting more than 5 seconds might constitute a risk even when it complies with the guidelines below". That is a conditional about unknown cumulative risk, stated as a possibility. It must not become a rule in flashmap, and it is recorded because a reader of our output should know the guidance itself says compliance is not the same as safety.

Appendix I, and a disagreement worth knowing about

The note's Appendix I gives a voltage to luminance table for a display of gamma 2.2 with peak white at 200 cd/m2, as an alternative to a spot photometer, and pins the two thresholds to it: 234 mV gives 20.1 cd/m2 and 631 mV gives 160 cd/m2.

flashmap reads luminance through the BT.1886 curve at peak 200, which is what BT.1702-3 and section 5 of this page specify. The two do not agree, and the gap is not small:

Luma, mV Appendix I, cd/m2 flashmap's BT.1886 at peak 200
100 3.8 1.87
234 20.1 14.42
400 61.0 52.21
631 160.0 155.91
700 200.0 200.00

Which direction each condition moves in. For the darker-bar condition our curve is the stricter of the two: a bar at 631 mV is exactly 160 by the table, which is not below it, and 155.91 by ours, which is. For the difference condition ours is the more permissive, and that is the direction that lets content through. A step that Appendix I calls exactly 20 cd/m2 reads on our curve as 15.68 cd/m2 from a darker level of 100 mV, 17.26 from 200 mV and 18.82 from 300 mV; above about 400 mV ours becomes the stricter one again.

Nothing has been changed on the strength of this. BT.1886 is what the recommendation in force asks for, and Appendix I describes itself as the typical relationship for a domestic display of the CRT era, offered for convenience when electrical measurement is easier than a photometer. They are two instruments, not two thresholds. But a reader comparing a flashmap verdict with a measurement made off Appendix I should expect them to disagree near the limit in the dark half of the range, and the size of it is in the table above.

Implementation decisions where the specs are silent

These are not thresholds. They are choices flashmap had to make to turn the wording above into code. Each one is named in the sidecar so that a consumer can see the reading that was used.

  1. How transitions are paired into flashes. WCAG says "A flash consists of two opposing transitions" but does not say whether a run of alternating transitions yields overlapping or non-overlapping pairs. flashmap pairs them non-overlapping, so a square wave at 5 Hz counts as 5 flashes per second and not 9. This matches the plain reading of "flashes more than three times in any one second period". Sidecar field: profile.transition_pairing.

  2. When a transition is declared. WCAG defines a transition between "adjacent peaks and valleys". A causal analyser cannot know a peak is a peak until the signal turns around. flashmap declares a transition on the first frame at which the swing away from the previous confirmed extreme meets the threshold, and then makes that value the new anchor. On a slow ramp this produces several same-direction transitions, which never pair into a flash, so a ramp does not count as flashing. Sidecar field: profile.transition_rule.

  3. Where the one-second window is placed. flashmap evaluates the trailing window [t - 1000 ms, t] at every frame timestamp t. If four flashes fall inside any one-second period then they also fall inside the trailing window that ends at the fourth of them, so the family of trailing windows is complete. Sidecar field: profile.window_placement.

  4. What "opposing" means for red. WCAG gives no sign for a colour transition. flashmap calls a transition into the saturated red state and a transition out of it opposing, which makes a red flash one entry plus one exit, both of which must move more than 0.2 in the CIE 1976 UCS diagram. Sidecar field: profile.red_opposing_rule.

  5. Where the flashing area is measured. On the picture, after letterbox and pillarbox bars are removed, never on the coded frame. Bars are not part of the picture and counting them would shrink every area fraction. Every sidecar carries picture.measured_on: "picture" and the bar geometry that was removed.

  6. The bar detector's own numbers (how dark a row has to be to count as a bar, how uniform it has to be, how many frames are sampled) are detector tuning, not thresholds from a spec. They live in picture.LetterboxSettings with their defaults written out, and the detected geometry is in the sidecar so a reviewer can check it by eye.

  7. Analysis resolution. Frames are scaled to a working width before analysis, which is a speed choice. Area fractions are ratios so they survive scaling. The size used is recorded in color.analysis_width and color.analysis_height.

  8. The room window is geometry, not a threshold. A 10 degree field at a seat distance d subtends 2 * d * tan(5 degrees). The 10 degrees is WCAG's, from section 2. The trigonometry is not a number that needs a source.

  9. How stripes are counted, and how the motion class is decided. Attachment 1 says "clearly discernible stripes in any orientation" and nothing more, so the detector had to be built out of choices rather than numbers. Each one is in pattern.PatternSettings with its default written out, and the whole block is in the sidecar under profile.pattern_rule.tuning, the same way the bar detector's numbers are under decision 6. None of them is a threshold:

    • stripes are counted along four scan families, rows, columns and the two diagonal shears, because the attachment allows any orientation and allows radial, curved and dotted patterns. A bar boundary is a swing that reaches the Guideline 1 flash difference with the darker level under the Guideline 1 darker image limit, read across the picture instead of across time, and only boundaries that oppose the one before them count, so a gradient across the screen counts no bars however far it travels. Twelve bars is six pairs;

    • the pattern area is the union of the stretches of those scanlines that lie between their outer bar boundaries, as a fraction of the picture;

    • motion is measured on the one dimensional stripe profile of the orientation that counted the most bars, not on the picture, because a grating can only be seen to move along its own normal. A shift is a whole pixel lag found by normalised cross correlation, searched out to just under one bar width. A pattern that travels a whole bar between two frames lands on top of itself, so the measurement is reported ambiguous rather than guessed at;

    • the smooth flow exemption fails safe. It is applied only when the flow is unambiguous: a full history of frame to frame comparisons, all in one direction, none still, none ambiguous, none below the match floor. Anything short of that is uncertain, and uncertain takes the 25% area, never the 40% one, and never the exemption. The cost is that a pattern between the two area limits is hidden for the first few frames, while the detector is still filling its history, and that a fine pattern flowing fast enough to alias is hidden rather than exempted. Every exemption that is applied is written into the map as its own interval in pattern_exemptions, so a reviewer sees what was let through instead of finding a silence. docs/DECISIONS.md carries the reasoning.

    • what makes a run of bars "regular". Guideline 2 is about regular patterns and the attachment counts pairs of stripes, but neither says how a detector decides that a run of light and dark steps repeats rather than merely alternates. Counting steps alone is not enough, and the case that proves it is text: cream on teal is 160.77 against 8.47 cd/m2, so every glyph edge clears the Guideline 1 difference and a scan line through a paragraph counts dozens of bars at no spacing in particular. Before this was added, every display frame of flashmap's own report page read as a pattern over 44 to 64 per cent of the screen.

      So a run of bars contributes area only when it passes two tests, because the guidance counts pairs of stripes, not signal strength. First a period: the unbiased autocorrelation, searched from two samples up to a third of the run, has a local maximum reaching min_autocorrelation_peak, and the smallest such lag is the period. Then consistent spacing: that period has to agree with the spacing of the boundaries that were actually counted, 2 * bar_width, to within spacing_tolerance. The second test is what makes the count a count of regular stripes rather than a count of edges on a line that happened to correlate somewhere. Four numbers, all detector tuning, none a threshold:

      Setting Default What it is
      require_periodicity true whether the two tests run at all. Off is for the ablation, and the map records which way it ran
      min_autocorrelation_peak 0.85 how strong the repeat has to be, 0 to 1
      min_cycles_for_period 3 how many whole cycles of a candidate period must fit in the run before that period is measurable
      spacing_tolerance 0.25 how far the period may sit from a whole multiple of 2 * bar_width

      A whole multiple, not the value itself, and the reason is worth keeping. Lags are whole samples and a real bar width is not. A twelve pair grating across 54 analysis rows is 2.22 samples a bar, so its period is 4.43 and no integer lag sits on it; the strongest integer lag is 9, the second harmonic, which is a period the signal really has. Comparing against 2 * bar_width alone reads that as a disagreement. Written that way it dropped five real gratings, every one of them horizontal, the worst a 32 pair grating covering 80 per cent of the screen, and the measurement caught it rather than a reviewer. MAX_HARMONIC is 3.

      min_cycles_for_period is deliberately well under the spec's pair count and must stay there. A twelve bar grating filling the picture spans five and a half periods, not six, because the run is measured from the first counted boundary to the last plus half a bar at each end, so a search insisting on six whole cycles could not find the period of the very first count the attachment can fail. The pair count is a gate of its own and is applied on its own.

      min_autocorrelation_peak was chosen by a rule fixed before the numbers were read, the largest peak at which recall does not fall, because a threshold picked to make one page pass is not a measurement. It has survived two changes to the suite since, which is the point of pre-registering it. results/pattern_text_precision.json holds the sweep over 171 rendered pages and gratings, including every type size and colour pair this product actually ships: recall is flat at 0.941 from 0 through 0.85 and drops above it, while precision goes from 0.246 with the tests off to 0.914 at 0.85. Not one of the 94 pages of text is called a pattern.

      What it does not fix, and this is written down rather than tuned away: body text at a fixed leading is a row of repetitive elements at a constant spacing, which Guideline 2 and the ITC note both say a pattern may be formed by, so a column scan through a paragraph can still have a period and it is the leading. One of the report page's nine display frames carries a finding for that reason, at 0.262 of the frame against a 0.25 limit, down from nine frames at 0.44 to 0.74. Nothing in either document exempts it and "clearly discernible" is a judgement neither supplies a number for, so flashmap reports it. The app's own 25 screens pass.

    There is no persistence time, no stripe width and no spatial frequency band in this list, because BT.1702-3 gives none and a vendor page is not a source (section 8.3).

One thing worth knowing about the two red numbers

The red flash test has two thresholds: R/(R + G + B) at or above 0.8, and a move of more than 0.2 in the CIE 1976 UCS diagram. Against a neutral background they overlap heavily. Measured over the whole sRGB cube, the furthest any colour with a red ratio between 0.5 and 0.78 can get from mid gray in that diagram is about 0.19, which is under the 0.2 limit. So for flashing between gray and a colour, lowering the ratio threshold anywhere down to roughly 0.45 changes nothing: the distance rule stops it first.

That is not a criticism of the spec. It matters for two practical reasons:

  • A consumer reading profile.red_flash should not assume the ratio alone decides anything.
  • A test on a generated clip cannot separate the two numbers, so flashmap pins them at unit level instead, in tests/test_flash_tracker.py. The geometry above is asserted there too, in test_the_two_red_thresholds_overlap_against_a_neutral_background, so the claim on this page is checked rather than believed.

Things we deliberately did not use

  • Any HDR rule. BT.1702-3's HDR branch and BT.2100 are out of scope for version 1. HLG and PQ tagged inputs are refused with a clear error rather than measured with the SDR curve.
  • The Harding FPA parameters, the Ofcom guidance note and the Japanese NAB/ARIB guidance. We could not read a primary copy of any of them, so no number from them appears in the code.
  • Any per-frame "risk score" or 0 to 100 scale. Nothing in either spec defines one.
